A starting list, not a recommendation: the strongest programs in your major at each level of reach for you. Nothing here is added to your list until you add it.

How this is built

  1. Who runs the program. Every school that reported bachelor's degrees in your major to the Department of Education last year. A school that graduated nobody in it is not a fit, whatever else it is.
  2. What the program is worth. Its graduates' earnings against everyone who finished the same field — measured against the school's own state where that figure exists, because in a field whose pay is regional the national comparison is mostly a statement about the state. Where earnings are unpublished, the size of the department and how central it is to the campus stand in, and they cannot carry a school as high as a measured result can.
  3. What it costs to carry. Median debt at graduation against median first-year pay.
  4. Where you can go. Three settings, because whether you can leave the state is not something this app can work out for you. Only schools in my state offers nothing outside it, however strong. Prefer my state holds half the list for it and lets the rest of the country compete for what is left — held across the whole list rather than per band, since a state's programs do not spread evenly across your odds, and a floor rather than a ceiling, so the ordinary ranking adds more wherever they earn it. Anywhere in the country stops counting residency rather than counting it against you. In-state publics rank above in-state privates throughout, since cost is the difference left once residency is settled, and the bands and the ranking are identical under all three.
  5. A spread, not a pile. Your odds sort the results into bands and the list is filled from each, so it comes out as four you should get into, four live chances and a few reaches — not twelve of whichever kind scored best.
  6. Caps. At most two primarily-online universities and three schools from any one state other than yours. Open-admission online schools otherwise take every safety place and leave you with no campus you can drive to.

Every input is the same public data used everywhere else in this app: modelled admission odds, and federal figures for the programs. None of it knows this year's department caps, what a campus is like, or whether you would be happy there.

      How the number is produced

      The pool, not the rate

      A school's transfer admit rate and the GPA its admitted transfers carry are enough to reconstruct the pool behind them. Admitting the top slice of a group lifts the admitted average by a known amount, so that arithmetic runs backwards: it recovers the average applicant, which is the person you are actually competing with.

      Your major sets the real rate

      The university rate is not the rate you face. Nursing and computer science admit a fraction of it and draw a stronger pool; classics and general studies admit above it. Each of the 154 majors carries both effects, plus whether the department admits directly, caps clinical seats, or weighs a portfolio.

      Where your file lands

      Your GPA and your file are placed inside that pool as a percentile, and admission sits at the percentile the seats run out. Everything on the file is measured against the typical applicant there, not against an empty page: being in-state at a state flagship is worth nothing when most of the pool is in-state too. Each field contributes exactly one line, so nothing is counted twice.

      Programs are reported, not ranked

      The Programs pane ranks nothing on reputation, because no reusable ranking of program quality exists to draw on. It reports four measured things and lets you sort on each: how many degrees a school awards in the field, how central the field is to that campus, what its graduates earn against everyone who finished the same field, and what they owe against what they earn. Earnings come from federal tax records, so they are counted rather than surveyed.

      Earnings read who enrolled as much as what was taught, and they are nominal — a school in an expensive city sits higher for that reason alone, which is why every field also carries its own state's median. Roughly a third of programs publish no earnings at all: the Department suppresses any figure drawn from too few graduates to report safely, and those show a size and nothing else rather than an estimate.

      Suggestions are a spread, not a pile

      Best fits puts the two halves of this app together: the odds decide which band a school competes in, and the program tables decide which schools lead that band. The list is then filled from every band rather than from the top, because twelve reaches is a bad list however strong the programs are. Two caps keep one kind of school from being the whole answer — at most two primarily-online universities, and three from any state but your own.

      Cost is the number nobody quotes

      The published price of a college is almost never what anyone pays. Net price — the price after the grant aid students actually received — is, and it moves enormously with household income: Harvard's sticker is $61,676 and its net price is $2,091 for a family earning $48,000 to $75,000. At a few very well-endowed schools the aid exceeds the entire cost of attendance and the lowest income bands are negative, meaning the student is paid to attend. Tell the record your income band and every card, suggestion and search result prices itself for you; leave it blank and you get the all-incomes average, labelled as such.

      The graduation rate shown beside it is the federal one, which counts first-time, full-time students only and therefore excludes transfer students by construction. It describes the campus you would be joining, not people arriving the way you are. It is worth knowing and it is not your rate; nothing here implies otherwise.

      Read it as a bracket

      P marks a published transfer admit rate, E one estimated from selectivity. The model does not know this year's department caps, and admission turns on things no calculator sees. Confirm every rate, deadline, and credit cap with the school.

      What this is, and what it is not

      It is a planning estimate. Every number on this page is modelled, not measured. No college publishes its transfer decisions at the applicant level, so nothing here is fitted to real outcomes — the model reconstructs each school's applicant pool from its published transfer admit rate and the GPA its admitted transfers carry, then places your record inside it. Treat a number as a bracket and a sanity check, never as a decision.

      It is not affiliated with any college. Institution names appear only to identify the schools the estimates describe. No school has reviewed, endorsed, or supplied anything here, and no logos or marks are used.

      It is not advice. Where you apply, what you major in, and what you spend on applications are your decisions. Confirm every admit rate, deadline, credit cap, and prerequisite with the school's own admissions office or its Common Data Set before you rely on it.

      Privacy

      Your record stays in your own browser's local storage. There is no account, no server, no database, and nothing is transmitted anywhere — the calculation runs entirely on your device. Clearing your browser data clears your record. Typefaces are served from this site rather than a font provider, so the page makes no third-party request at all.

      Cost and completion figures

      Net price, published tuition, graduation and retention rates come from the U.S. Department of Education's College Scorecard institution file, which is in the public domain. Net price is the total cost of attendance minus the grant and scholarship aid students actually received, reported both as an average and split across five household income bands; the app uses the band you give it and falls back to the average, saying which it used. Graduation is the six-year rate for first-time, full-time students and excludes transfers. Roughly 99% of the schools here carry all of it.

      Program figures

      Degrees awarded, graduate earnings and graduate debt come from the U.S. Department of Education's College Scorecard field-of-study file, which is in the public domain. Earnings are the median for graduates of that program at that school one and two years after completing, drawn from federal tax records, and cover students who received federal aid. Schools are matched to the federal record by IPEDS UNITID, and each of this app's majors is mapped to the federal CIP field it falls in — where a major spans two fields, both are pooled and the app says so. Nothing in that pane is modelled or estimated: a figure is either the federal one or it is marked unpublished.

      Where the numbers come from

      Each school carries one transfer admit rate and one admitted-transfer GPA. P marks a rate taken from a published transfer figure; E marks one estimated from overall selectivity and peer institutions. Both are approximations compiled for planning. Corrections are welcome and easy to apply — the reference table is a plain text file.

      1. What the service is

      Matriculate helps a prospective transfer student build a list of colleges worth applying to. It does several distinct things, and they carry different weight:

      • Admission estimates. The likelihood that an applicant with a given record would be admitted. Every one of these numbers is modelled, not measured. No college publishes transfer decisions at the applicant level, so nothing here is fitted to real outcomes. The model reconstructs each school's applicant pool from its published transfer admit rate and the grade point average its admitted transfers carry, then places your record inside that pool.
      • Best fits. A suggested slate assembled from those estimates and the preferences you give it. A suggestion, not a recommendation.
      • Cost, completion and program figures. Net price, tuition, graduation and retention rates, degrees awarded, graduate earnings and graduate debt. These are reproduced from federal data as published — not modelled by this site.
      • Deadlines. Verified system-wide filing periods where they exist, and otherwise a period rather than a date.
      • Transfer guarantees. Summaries of statewide articulation policy for the states where one was read from a primary source, with a link to that source, plus links into ASSIST for California.

      Estimates are planning aids. They are not predictions, guarantees, or statements of fact about any admission decision.

      3. Accuracy of the estimates

      4. Accuracy of the federal figures

The federal graduation rate counts only first-time, full-time students and therefore excludes transfer students by construction — it describes the campus, not people arriving the way you are. Graduate earnings cover federally aided students and reflect cohorts who finished several years ago.

      Privacy

      Last updated 18 September 2026

      This site collects nothing. There is no account, no server, no database, no analytics, no advertising, no tracking pixel and no cookie. Every calculation runs inside your browser, on your device. Nothing you type is transmitted anywhere, because there is nowhere for it to go.

      What is stored, and where

      Your record — grade point average, credits, college, residency, household income band, major, entry term, self-ratings and your school list — is written to your browser's local storage under the key matriculate:v1. A second key, matriculate:walkthrough:v1, records only that you have seen the opening questions, so you are not asked again. Earlier versions of the site used transfer-odds:v1 and transfer-odds:walkthrough:v1; those are read once so a returning visitor does not lose their list, and are otherwise unused.

      Local storage lives on your own device. It is not a cookie, it is never sent with a network request, and no one else can read it, including the publisher. Your income band is deliberately left out of shared links — a link you send someone carries your grades and your school list, never what your household earns. This is enforced by a test that fails the build if income ever survives a round trip through a link.

      To erase it: press Clear on the record panel, or clear site data for this domain in your browser settings. A private or incognito window keeps nothing after you close it. Export CSV writes a file to your own machine and sends it nowhere.

      Under the ePrivacy Directive and comparable rules, storage a user explicitly asks for — here, your own record, saved so the site is usable on your next visit — is strictly necessary for the function requested and needs no consent banner. That is the only storage this site performs.

      Third parties

      The site contacts none. The typefaces are served from this site's own origin rather than from a font CDN, so while you use the site your browser makes no request to any third party, and no third party sees your address through it. This was verified by recording every network request the site makes during a full session: all of them go to this site and nowhere else.

      The site does link to other places — colleges' own admissions pages, federal records, state policy pages and ASSIST. Nothing is sent to them until you click, and what you type here is never attached to those links. Once you follow one you are on someone else's site, under their privacy policy rather than this one.

      Offline use

      A service worker stores the site's own files on your device so it keeps working without a connection, and there is a single-file offline copy you can download and keep. Both hold the site itself, never your record, and neither sends anything anywhere.

      Server logs

      The site is static files on a hosting provider. Like any web host, that provider may record standard request logs — address, timestamp, page requested, user agent — for delivery, security and abuse prevention. The publisher does not analyse those logs to identify or profile visitors.

      Your record is yours

      Because nothing is collected, there is nothing to request, correct, export or delete from anyone else's systems — the rights you hold under the GDPR, the UK GDPR, the CCPA and similar laws have no data here to attach to. Nothing is ever sold or shared, because nothing is ever received.
